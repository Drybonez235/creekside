# Google Ads Keyword Match Types: Why We Default to Broad Match (And When We Switch to Phrase)

Most guides treat Google Ads keyword match types as a maturity decision. You start narrow with exact match for control, then gradually open up to phrase, and finally try broad match once you feel ready. That framing is backwards from how we actually run accounts.

Based on $20M+ in managed ad spend across service businesses and e-commerce accounts, the match type decision is not about your risk tolerance. It is about two specific signals from your own campaign setup: your budget and what you found during pre-launch keyword research. Here is how Creekside Marketing actually approaches this.

## What Each Google Ads Keyword Match Type Does

The differences between broad, phrase, and exact match are worth reviewing before getting into when to use each one. Most accounts that struggle with match types treat the choice as a one-time permanent decision rather than a signal-driven one that should reflect where the campaign is right now and what data is available.

**Exact match** shows your ad when someone searches for your exact keyword plus close variants and misspellings. It is the most restrictive option. We reach for exact match only when targeting a very specific product or a very specific type of customer who would search for that precise term. Outside that narrow scenario, exact match collects data too slowly for most new campaigns that still need to figure out what is actually working.

**Phrase match** requires your keyword to appear in the search query in the correct order. Other words can come before or after. It gives meaningful control while still allowing enough search variation to collect useful performance data.

**Broad match** shows your ad across the widest range of searches Google considers related to your keyword. It surfaces traffic you would not have anticipated, which is both the opportunity and the risk.

## The Two Factors That Should Drive Your Match Type Decision

For most new campaigns, the question is not which match type is "best" in the abstract. It is which match type makes sense given the resources and data you have available right now. According to Creekside Marketing's analysis across dozens of accounts, two factors from your campaign's own setup determine the answer: your budget size and the number of negative keywords you uncovered during pre-launch keyword research.

**Budget size.** A larger budget can afford to explore. Broad match generates high search term volume, including terms that will not convert. On a larger budget, you run that exploratory phase, review the search term report closely, add negatives as irrelevant terms appear, and identify which search patterns are actually driving results. That feedback loop is valuable. On a smaller budget, every wasted click has a higher relative cost, and the margin for exploratory spend is much thinner. That is when phrase match earns its place. It narrows the field before you spend rather than after.

**Negative keyword count from pre-launch research.** The number of negative keywords you found during keyword research, using tools like SEMrush, is a direct signal of how much filtering your campaign needs before it goes live. Here is the part most guides miss: a high negative keyword count from research actually makes broad match more viable, not less. When you have done thorough pre-launch research and loaded your account with a strong negative list, broad match can explore within a much cleaner environment. You have already filtered the most predictable bad traffic before the campaign starts.

A low negative count cuts both ways. It might mean the keyword is naturally precise and broad match will behave well. Or it might mean the research was not deep enough. Either way, phrase match is the safer starting point until you have real search term data to evaluate.

## Our Actual Default: Broad Match When Conditions Allow

Creekside Marketing defaults to broad match when budget and research conditions support it. This is a stronger preference than most guides will give you, and it comes from running campaigns across a wide range of accounts and industries over multiple years of active management.

The reason is straightforward: broad match surfaces useful data faster. When you are actively managing a campaign in its first few weeks, you can check the search term report frequently, add negatives as irrelevant terms appear, and identify which search patterns are driving real results. That feedback loop accelerates performance in a way that phrase or exact match cannot match early on, because they limit the search volume you see during the critical data collection window.

We pair that broad match preference with a non-negotiable precondition: a negative keyword list built before launch. The pre-launch research phase exists specifically to prevent bad clicks from day one. Broad match without that pre-built list is a budget leak. Broad match with it is a data engine.

## When to Use a Mix of Both Match Types

Most real campaigns use both broad and phrase, and that is usually the right call. A mix lets you balance data collection speed against the cost of irrelevant clicks, applying the match type that fits each individual keyword's situation rather than forcing a uniform approach across the entire campaign.

A keyword where pre-launch research produced a long negative list gets phrase match. A keyword where research came back relatively clean gets broad match. This keyword-level decision, rather than a campaign-wide blanket setting, lets you collect data faster on the cleaner keywords while staying disciplined on the ones more likely to pull in off-target traffic. There is no single correct answer. The right mix is the one that fits your specific budget, industry, and how deeply you did your pre-launch research.

## Exact Match: The Narrow Use Case

Exact match has a clear role in Google Ads, but a narrow one. Use it when the campaign needs to show up for one specific search term and nothing else, with no variation. Very specific products with highly distinct names, niche service terms with tight intent, or conquest campaigns targeting a competitor's brand name: those are the conditions where exact match belongs. You are not trying to collect data broadly. You are trying to control spend against a known specific target.

For most new campaigns building their data foundation, exact match creates too much restriction too early. The standard approach is to collect search term data from broad and phrase first, identify which terms consistently convert, and then layer in exact match on those proven queries later in the campaign lifecycle.

## How Match Types Connect to Negative Keyword Research

The relationship between pre-launch negative keyword research and match type selection is one of the most underappreciated dynamics in Google Ads campaign setup. The work you do before launch directly determines how aggressively you can run broad match once the campaign is live. Without that research, broad match is risky. With it, broad match is an accelerant.

We research negative keywords before launch specifically to prevent bad clicks from day one, not as an afterthought to be handled once spend has already been wasted. That investment in pre-launch filtering is what makes the broad-match-first approach workable across a wide range of industries and budget levels.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I use broad match or exact match for a new Google Ads campaign?**
For most new campaigns, skip exact match entirely. Choose between broad and phrase based on your budget and how many negative keywords you found during pre-launch research. Exact match limits data collection too much during the early stage when you are still identifying which search terms actually convert for your business.

**Does broad match waste budget in Google Ads?**
It can, particularly on small budgets or when you launch without a pre-built negative keyword list. Broad match works best when paired with active management and solid pre-launch research. On a larger budget with a strong negative list in place, broad match accelerates data collection and surfaces high-performing search patterns faster than phrase or exact.

**What is the difference between broad match and phrase match in Google Ads?**
Broad match shows your ad for searches Google considers related to your keyword, including synonyms and implied intent, with no required word order. Phrase match requires your keyword phrase to appear in the correct order within the search query, with other words allowed before or after. Phrase match is more controlled. Broad match generates more reach and more early-stage data.

**When should I switch from broad match to phrase match?**
Two signals: your budget is too small to absorb exploratory spend, or your pre-launch keyword research produced a high number of negative keywords, meaning the keyword has a lot of adjacent bad traffic. Either condition makes phrase match the safer starting point.

**Can I use both broad and phrase match in the same Google Ads campaign?**
Yes, and that is often the right approach. Applying match types at the keyword level, rather than uniformly across the campaign, lets you balance data collection speed against click quality on a keyword-by-keyword basis. In many accounts, a keyword-level mix outperforms committing to one match type across the board.
