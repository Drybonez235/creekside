## Why a Google Ads Specialist Outperforms a Generalist Managing Both Platforms

Here's the thing about paid advertising most agencies won't say out loud: Google Ads and Meta Ads are not two versions of the same skill. The person who is genuinely excellent at one is rarely excellent at both. The platforms reward different analytical instincts, different optimization rhythms, and different technical knowledge. You need specialists, not one person stretched thin across two very different systems.

A Google Ads specialist lives inside intent signals, keyword strategy, bid management, conversion tracking architecture, and negative keyword hygiene. A Meta Ads specialist lives inside audience construction, creative fatigue cycles, pixel health, and cold traffic funnel behavior. These skill sets overlap at the edges but diverge significantly in the daily optimization work.

Creekside Marketing was built around this reality from the start: Peterson handles Google Ads, Cade handles Meta Ads. The two have been working together for four years. According to Peterson, "If you go with someone who says they're a jack of all trades, typically that means master of none." That is not a slogan. It is a pattern Creekside has observed while auditing accounts that were underperforming.

Based on Creekside Marketing's analysis of $20M+ in managed ad spend across 200+ audited accounts, the structural problem in underperforming inherited accounts is consistent: one person managing both platforms without the depth to optimize either one well.

## What Two Specialists Actually Look Like When They Work Together

When you have one person dedicated to Google Ads and one dedicated to Meta Ads, the work gets divided by actual expertise rather than available time. Creekside's model pairs a mechanical engineer on the Google side with an MBA-trained accountant on the Meta side, four years into working together on the same client accounts. The depth in each platform comes from specialization, not from splitting attention.

Peterson came from mechanical engineering. Cade completed an MBA with an accounting background. Both ended up in paid advertising because they are numbers-first thinkers, and both noticed early that marketing agencies often lack the ability to find and act on the relevant data inside a campaign. According to Peterson, "that's something that a lot of marketing agencies lack, the ability to find relevant data and find relevant insights in their marketing campaigns to improve upon them."

Those backgrounds are not incidental. Google Ads optimization is, at its core, a structural and statistical problem: match types, bid strategy sequencing, quality score factors, negative keyword compounding over time. The discipline of thinking like an engineer, systematic, iterative, pattern-driven, translates directly. Meta Ads optimization involves understanding creative fatigue, audience overlap, and how cold traffic behaves at different funnel stages. The financial modeling and behavioral analysis you develop in an MBA program shapes that kind of thinking.

The two working together produce what Peterson calls a "well-oiled machine." The clients Creekside works with include both business owners and larger agencies, including Duct Tape Marketing, one of the most recognized small business marketing brands in the country. For agencies that want strong paid ads results without hiring two sets of deep expertise in-house, the two-specialist model solves a real structural problem.

## The Tracking Foundation Every Google Ads Specialist Checks First

Before any specialist can do meaningful optimization work, the tracking foundation has to be solid. This is what generalists most often miss, and when they miss it, every subsequent optimization is working off bad data. The ad platforms are optimizing toward whatever signal you give them. If that signal is wrong, you are spending money to get better at the wrong thing.

"If you've bad data, you're going to have bad results. And so, that's one of the key foundations of how we improve results for our clients," Peterson says. Creekside uses Google Tag Manager to set up conversion tracking correctly on both Google and Meta. In a significant portion of the 200+ accounts Creekside has audited, something in the tracking setup is broken: conversions double-counting, wrong actions flagged as conversions, tags not firing at all.

A Google Ads specialist who has built and audited tracking setups dozens of times knows exactly where the failure points hide. A Meta Ads specialist who has verified pixel health across many accounts knows what correct implementation looks like. A generalist managing both platforms under time pressure tends to prioritize campaign-level work over the unglamorous tracking verification that determines whether any of that campaign work means anything.

Look, if your tracking is broken and nobody has audited it recently, everything in your account is optimization theater. The Creekside audit process begins with tracking verification before touching a single campaign setting. That sequence is not arbitrary.

## What the Audit Reveals About Accounts That Had Generalists Running Them

Creekside's audit checklist was developed after systematically reviewing accounts where campaigns were failing and identifying what went wrong. The checklist covers tracking configuration, campaign structure, bidding setup, match type hygiene, audience settings, and reporting quality. It finds the specific error before recommending any changes, because fixing the wrong thing first is a real risk when a previous manager has layered months of competing decisions into an account.

According to Peterson, "if you are currently facing issues in an account that you paid a supposed expert to run and it's not doing well, we will find the error. We developed this process ourselves after auditing dozens of accounts to get results for customers."

After the audit comes the 90-day plan. Cade describes it: "we come up with a 90-day plan of what we're going to do, how we're going to implement everything, and how you can expect to achieve better results." Not a vague roadmap. A structured plan with specific changes, timelines, and the criteria for knowing whether progress is real.

## The Questions That Separate a Real Google Ads Specialist From a Generalist

Most paid ads managers call themselves specialists. The questions below cut through that quickly. A real Google Ads specialist gives specific answers about tracking setup, bid strategy sequencing, and what their current client work actually consists of. A generalist gives general answers. The difference is obvious when you know what to ask for.

**What percentage of your current client work is Google Ads versus Meta Ads?** The answer tells you where the depth is. A Google Ads specialist has most of their active work on that platform, not split evenly across two platforms and other channels.

**What does your tracking audit process look like?** A specialist who has set up Google Tag Manager for conversions across dozens of accounts has a specific answer about where failures hide and what correct implementation looks like. Vague answers here mean vague results later.

**What does a 90-day plan include?** The answer should name specific structural changes, the timeline for each, and the criteria for knowing whether progress is real. Generic roadmap language is a warning sign.

**Will we always have full access to our account?** This should be answered upfront without you having to ask. If you have to push for it, that is useful information about how the relationship will go.

## Why the Specialist Model Holds Up Across a Portfolio of Clients

Running multiple client accounts without specialist-level depth creates compounding errors. The organizational systems that support specialization, including account tracking and daily checklists, are what prevent one client's situation from affecting how another client's account gets managed. This is less visible than campaign settings but just as important.

When Peterson manages multiple Google Ads clients, a tracking system keeps every client's account activity completely separate. "You may ask, hey, you work with a bunch of clients. How do you keep them all separate? Here's exactly how," Peterson says in the video. The management spreadsheet shows what actions have been taken in each account, what matters in each, and keeps the clients distinct from each other. One client's budget pacing issue does not bleed into how you think about another client's bid strategy.

Cade's daily and weekly checklists work the same way on the Meta side. When you are managing multiple accounts, the checklist is what prevents important signals from being missed because something else was demanding attention that day.

The specialist model, supported by those management systems, is what allows Creekside to work with agencies like Duct Tape Marketing alongside direct client relationships without degraded results. The depth comes from specialization. The reliability comes from the systems built around it.

Across the 200+ accounts Creekside has audited with $20M+ in managed spend, the pattern is consistent: accounts underperform when one person is stretched across two platforms. The specialist model exists specifically because that pattern is predictable, and because fixing it requires genuine depth, not better effort from a generalist.
