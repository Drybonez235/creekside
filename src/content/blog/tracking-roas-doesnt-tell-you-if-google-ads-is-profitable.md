Not rhetorically. If someone asked you today, could you point to a number?

Peterson Rainey, who manages $20M+ in ad spend at Creekside Marketing, frames the problem directly: most businesses burning cash on paid ads have no idea which direction they are heading. ## Why ROAS Doesn't Tell You If Google Ads Is Profitable

ROAS measures revenue efficiency, not profitability. It shows how much revenue you generated per ad dollar spent, and that's the only question it answers. A 4x ROAS means four dollars back for every dollar in. But it says nothing about what actually determines profitability: customer lifetime value, repeat purchase behavior, or how many of those customers stay long enough to reach their projected value.

Tracking ROAS alone and treating it as a profitability signal creates the same confusion as watching revenue trend upward while assuming net income must be improving. The signals are related. They are not the same.

Look, ROAS matters. We track it on every account. But if someone asks whether your paid advertising is actually profitable, ROAS is the starting point for that answer, not the end.

## What the Pilot Phase in Google Ads Is Actually Doing

The pilot phase is the testing and signal-gathering period at the start of a campaign. The algorithm distributes spend to discover what audiences and placements convert for your business. ROAS will be lower than your eventual optimized baseline. That's expected, not a sign of failure.

Most businesses misread the pilot phase entirely. They see a lower ROAS during the first several weeks and interpret it as the campaign not working. They cut budgets, restructure campaigns, or switch agencies. Each of those decisions resets the data collection period and pushes the optimized baseline further out.

The right question during the pilot phase is not whether ROAS is where you want it. The right question is whether the trajectory puts you on a path to where you need to be. A profitability model gives you a specific ROAS or CAC target ahead of time, so you're evaluating trajectory against a benchmark rather than reacting to a snapshot that doesn't represent optimized performance.

## How Customer Lifespan and Repeat Purchase Rate Change the Break-Even Calculation

Customer lifespan and repeat purchase rate are the two inputs that change the break-even calculation most. Standard ROAS reporting ignores both. If a customer buys once and leaves, their value is one transaction minus acquisition cost. If they purchase multiple times over the course of their relationship with your business, the return on that acquisition is a completely different number.

The break-even calculation shifts substantially once repeat rate and customer lifespan are factored in. A customer acquisition cost that looks high against a single transaction may be entirely reasonable once you account for the repeat purchases that follow. The reverse holds too. If your actual repeat rate is lower than your planning assumption, a CAC that appeared profitable at launch will not hold up over time.

A profitability model requires these inputs because without them you are calculating break-even against a single-transaction model. That model may or may not reflect how your customers actually behave.

## CAC vs LTV Across Low, Mid, and High Performance Scenarios

Your paid ads performance will not stay constant, and that's the reason Peterson's profitability framework models CAC vs LTV across three scenarios rather than one. The question isn't what your customer acquisition cost looks like when everything works well. It's what your profitability looks like across the realistic range of outcomes.

Competitor bids shift. Audiences saturate. Seasonality moves conversion rates. Performance in any given month reflects a blend of these factors, not just campaign quality. A business that is profitable only at peak performance is fragile. A business that generates acceptable returns even in the low-performance scenario has something durable.

Running three scenarios rather than one also calibrates how you respond to normal variance. If the model shows you're profitable even at low performance, a down month is context. If you're only profitable at peak, a down period is a genuine problem. Knowing which situation you're in, before you start spending, changes how you make decisions throughout the campaign.

## Actual Profit After Accounting for Churn

Churn is the variable that most frequently creates a gap between theoretical profitability and actual profitability. Any customer lifetime value calculation assumes customers stay for a certain period. Churn is the rate at which they leave before reaching that point. If your model assumes a longer customer relationship and your actual churn produces a shorter one, your projected lifetime value is overstated. Every customer acquired at the break-even CAC built on that assumption is actually below break-even once real retention data is applied.

For subscription and recurring revenue models, accounting for churn is not optional. The projected lifetime value is the ceiling. Churn-adjusted lifetime value is the number your profitability calculation should be built on.

For ecommerce businesses, churn shows up differently. It appears as reduced repeat purchase rates and shortened customer relationships. The profitability model accounts for both structures.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a higher ROAS mean Google Ads is profitable?**

Not necessarily. ROAS measures revenue per ad dollar spent, not profit. Profitability depends on margin structure, how often customers come back, how long they stay, and how many churn before reaching their expected lifetime value. A high ROAS campaign can be unprofitable. A moderate ROAS campaign with strong repeat behavior and low churn can be highly profitable. The full picture requires modeling pilot phase trajectory, CAC vs LTV, and churn-adjusted profit alongside ROAS.

**What is the pilot phase and why does ROAS look worse during it?**

The pilot phase is the early testing and learning period at the start of a campaign. During this time, the algorithm distributes spend to identify what audiences and placements convert for your specific business. The campaign generates signal alongside revenue, which is why ROAS is lower than the eventual optimized baseline. This is expected behavior. Cutting spend or restructuring during the pilot phase resets the data collection period and delays reaching that optimized baseline.

**What inputs does a paid ads profitability model require?**

A complete profitability model requires: repeat purchase rate, customer churn rate, purchase frequency, average transaction value or monthly recurring revenue, and monthly ad budget. With those inputs, you can forecast pilot phase expectations versus optimized performance, CAC vs LTV across low, mid, and high performance scenarios, and churn-adjusted profit projections before committing to a spend level.

**Why does modeling CAC vs LTV across scenarios matter more than tracking peak ROAS?**

Peak ROAS shows what's possible when everything works. Modeling CAC vs LTV across low, mid, and high performance scenarios shows what your business economics look like across the realistic range of outcomes. A business that's profitable only at peak performance is fragile. Running three scenarios instead of one gives you a model that tells you whether a down period is context or a genuine problem, so you can calibrate your response rather than reacting on instinct.
