# Stop Managing Google Ads From Memory: The Weekly Checklist System That Keeps Any Account on Track

If you are managing Google Ads across more than a handful of accounts -- or if you have a freelancer sharing management duties with you -- you have probably hit the same problem. The account looks fine. But when something shifts, nobody can tell you what specifically changed, when it changed, or whether there was a test running at the time.

We built a Google Ads management checklist to fix that. This post is based on a video Peterson published on the Creekside Marketing YouTube channel: [How To Use Our Google Ads Management Spreadsheet Checklist - 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y0-LOwiSDZ0). It walks through every section of the spreadsheet Creekside uses internally across all accounts -- including the test tracking section, which Peterson calls the most important piece of the whole thing.

This is not a generic best-practices article. It is the specific system Creekside runs, built from managing $20M+ in ad spend, to keep accounts consistent whether Peterson or a freelancer is in the seat on any given week.

## Why a Google Ads Management Checklist Beats a Dashboard Alone

Google Ads dashboards show you what is happening right now. They are not designed to show you what changed last week, what someone tested three months ago, or why the account looks different than it did before the last person managed it.

The checklist solves a different problem than the dashboard. Per Creekside's management system, the purpose of the spreadsheet is to track changes and adjustments being made in an account week to week -- so that everyone involved, whether the account manager or a freelancer, stays on the same page about what has been accomplished and what still needs to happen.

The dashboard tells you the current state. The checklist tells you the decision history.

When you are managing multiple accounts or handing off to a freelancer, that decision history is what prevents repeated mistakes, duplicate tests, and results that cannot be explained because the reasoning was never written down.

## Section 1: Standard Weekly Checks (Run at Minimum Twice Per Week)

The checklist opens with a set of standard checks that apply to every account every week. According to Creekside's management system, these checks need to be completed at minimum twice a week -- no exceptions.

The standard recurring checks are:

**Search term exclusions.** Reviewing the search terms report to identify new negative keyword candidates. [Reviewing search terms for negative keywords](/how-to-review-search-terms-and-add-negative-keywords-without-tanking-your-google-ads-account/) is non-negotiable regardless of account maturity or campaign type. Terms that trigger the wrong intent cost money every day they stay active.

**Optimization score.** Reviewing Google's recommendation panel. This is not about blindly accepting every suggestion -- it is about making deliberate, documented decisions on what to apply and what to dismiss. Ignoring it entirely is as risky as auto-applying everything.

**Bid adjustments.** Checking audience, location, and campaign type bid adjustments to see if any need updating based on recent performance trends. Bid adjustments decay in relevance quickly as audience behavior shifts.

**Ad schedules.** Reviewing the current schedule to see whether it should be narrowed (to cut spend during consistently low-converting hours) or expanded (if the schedule is too conservative and cutting off volume during productive times).

**Search keyword review.** Especially relevant for manual CPC campaigns -- checking whether keywords need raised top-of-page bids based on current auction data. Falling below the top-of-page threshold on high-intent keywords is a common and invisible profit leak.

**Campaign and ad group comparison.** Looking across campaigns and ad groups side by side to spot meaningful performance differences. One ad group dramatically outperforming another is a signal worth investigating and acting on.

On top of these standard checks, Creekside builds business-specific ancillary checks into each client's spreadsheet. These are documented per account and apply only to that specific business -- not every account needs the same secondary tasks.

The checkbox format keeps the system honest. When two checks are completed per week, every standard check should have a mark next to it. If boxes are consistently left unchecked, that is a visible and documented signal that something is being skipped -- not an assumption that it probably got done.

## Section 2: Monthly Budget and Target CPA

The budget section tracks the monthly spending goal broken down on a 30-day basis. The more important element is the target CPA -- the single number that determines whether an account is on track, needs routine attention, or requires a major overhaul. With both figures documented in the same spreadsheet as the weekly checks, there is no ambiguity about what the account is supposed to accomplish.

The target CPA is the more critical element. According to Creekside's documented system, the target CPA is how we determine three things:

1. Whether the account is currently performing well
2. Whether it needs routine maintenance
3. Whether it needs a major overhaul

When an account is not hitting target CPA -- or is clearly not on pace to hit it within the current billing cycle -- that is the specific trigger to escalate. Not a vague "things feel off" check-in, but a documented flag: off target by this amount, here is the gap, what is the response?

Having the target CPA written into the same spreadsheet as the weekly checks removes the ambiguity that erodes client trust. There is no "I think we were aiming for around $80" -- the number is in the document, visible to every person who manages the account.

## Section 3: Notes (Manager Context and Freelancer Observations, Kept Separate)

The notes section has two distinct parts that are deliberately kept separate: manager notes, which capture stable standing context about the account, and freelancer notes, which serve as a live working log. The distinction matters because stable context that gets overwritten by working notes creates confusion -- and confused freelancers make expensive decisions on faulty assumptions.

**Manager notes** are Peterson's standing overview of the account: what Creekside is optimizing toward, the context a new person needs to understand the account quickly, and any account-specific characteristics that would otherwise take a freelancer weeks to discover through trial and error. These notes are designed to stay stable. They are the essential reading anyone needs before touching the account.

**Freelancer notes** are a working log. The freelancer uses this section however they need -- to capture observations, flag questions, and document anything that does not fit cleanly into the other sections. This gives the person managing the account a place to think out loud in a way that Peterson can review later.

The structure is deliberate: stable context stays stable, live observations stay separate. When Peterson hands an account to a freelancer, the freelancer does not need to ask what the background is or what has already been tried. It is in the manager notes. When Peterson reviews an account after a freelancer has been managing it, he has a log of their observations alongside the standard metrics.

## Section 4: Action Items

Action items are the discrete, finite task list for the account: concrete changes the team wants to make, such as adding image assets, updating headline copy, creating a sitelink, or adjusting a landing page. Each action item gets completed and checked off. Nothing carries over as an indefinite open loop -- that is what the test tracking section handles.

They are discrete tasks -- they get completed and checked off.

The distinction between action items and the test tracking section matters. Action items are finite tasks. Tests are ongoing changes that affect campaign-level performance and require monitoring over time. A change to a bid strategy is a test. Updating an ad's copy is an action item. Conflating the two is how teams end up running experiments with no start dates and no criteria for what a determination actually looks like.

## Section 5: Test Tracking (The Most Important Section)

Peterson calls the test tracking section the most important part of the entire checklist. It is a color-coded log of every test, experiment, and significant campaign change -- each with a start date and a minimum two-week evaluation window. The four status colors (blue, green, red, white) let anyone reviewing the account see immediately what is running, what worked, what failed, and what needs revisiting.

Every test, experiment, or significant change gets logged here with a start date. The color-coding system tracks status at a glance:

- **Blue** -- active (the test is live and currently running)
- **Green** -- working (a positive determination has been made, the approach is confirmed)
- **Red** -- failed (the test did not work, the variation was turned off)
- **White** -- inconclusive (the test ended, but not enough data was collected to make a confident call -- flagged to revisit when conditions allow)

Each entry records a start date. The minimum evaluation window per Creekside's system is two weeks. For accounts with lower conversion volume, that window can extend to six to eight weeks. The goal is collecting enough data to distinguish a real signal from randomness.

Closing a test too early is a specific mistake this system is designed to prevent. If you make an account-level decision after one week on a low-volume account, you are reacting to noise, not data.

One important detail from the Creekside checklist: marking a test as finished does not mean the test stopped running. A green test may still be running -- it just means the team made a determination that it works and is keeping it. A red test means the variation was turned off. A white test means the results were inconclusive and the team wants to revisit when data improves.

Per Creekside's system, any change that causes Google to re-enter a learning phase belongs in the test tracking section. This is not limited to formally structured A/B experiments. A bid strategy change, a new campaign type, a major audience restructure -- any of these can affect the learning phase, and any of them deserve a logged start date and an open evaluation window.

The section also functions as a forward-looking backlog. Future tests the team wants to run get added with the start date left blank. When there is bandwidth to start a new experiment, the ideas are already documented. The team pulls from a tested backlog instead of brainstorming from scratch during an account review.

## How the System Works in Practice

The Google Ads management checklist exists for one reason: handoff consistency. When Peterson manages an account one week and a freelancer manages it the next, neither person loses the institutional knowledge that makes the account run correctly. The manager notes, test log, action items, and target CPA are all documented -- not in someone's head. When Peterson manages an account one week and a freelancer manages it the next, neither party loses the institutional knowledge that makes the account run correctly.

According to Creekside's documented process, the spreadsheet should be updated at minimum twice per week. The standard weekly checks get completed on each update. The test tracking section and action items get updated as changes happen in the account.

The manager notes section means a freelancer stepping into an account for the first time does not need to ask: what is the goal? What are the quirks of this account? What has already been tried? It is there.

The test log means nobody has to remember what changed in a previous month and whether it worked. It is documented, color-coded, and searchable.

This is how [systematic paid ads account management](/paid-ads-agency-client-onboarding-checklist/) avoids the slow performance decay that happens when decisions are made by memory -- and when the person who remembers the reasoning is no longer managing the account.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How often should the Google Ads management checklist be updated?**

At minimum twice per week. The standard checks -- search term exclusions, optimization score, bid adjustments, ad schedules, keyword review, and campaign comparisons -- should be completed on each update cycle. The test tracking section should be updated any time a new test is started, an existing test is closed out, or a change is made that could push the account into a learning phase.

**What is the difference between action items and the test tracking section?**

Action items are discrete, finite tasks: add an image asset, update an ad, build a sitelink. They get completed and checked off. The test tracking section is for ongoing changes that affect campaign-level performance -- experiments, new campaign types, bid strategy shifts -- that require monitoring over time and a formal determination before they close. If a change causes Google to re-enter a learning phase, it is a test entry, not an action item.

**How long should a test run before making a determination?**

Creekside's standard is a minimum of two weeks. For accounts with lower conversion volume, that extends to six to eight weeks. The goal is collecting enough data to distinguish a real result from randomness. Closing a test after one week on a low-volume account means making decisions on noise.

**Does marking a test as "finished" mean it stopped running?**

No. Per Creekside's system, "finished" means a determination was made. A green test may still be running -- the determination was that it works and the approach is being kept. A red test means the failing variation was turned off. A white test means results were inconclusive and the team wants to revisit when conditions are better.

**Can this system work when multiple people are managing the same account?**

Yes. That is specifically what it is designed for. Manager notes capture the standing context that stays stable regardless of who is managing. Freelancer notes are a working log for whoever is managing at a given time. The test log and action items are shared across both. Any qualified person can step into the account without losing the context that makes it run correctly.

## Key Takeaways

The Creekside Google Ads management spreadsheet checklist runs on every account, updated at minimum twice per week. The five sections are:

1. **Standard weekly checks** -- search term exclusions, optimization score, bid adjustments, ad schedules, keyword review, campaign and ad group comparisons
2. **Monthly budget and target CPA** -- the number that determines whether the account is performing, needs maintenance, or needs a full overhaul
3. **Manager and freelancer notes** -- stable standing context and a live working log, kept in separate sections so neither overwrites the other
4. **Action items** -- the discrete task list for the account; completed items get checked off
5. **Test tracking** -- the most important section; every experiment gets a start date, a color-coded status, and a minimum two-week evaluation window

The test tracking section is what separates managed accounts from monitored ones. It turns account management from a memory exercise into a documented process -- one that any qualified person can inherit without losing what made the account work.
